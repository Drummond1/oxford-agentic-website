Most AI training now describes itself in the same words: practical, hands-on, applied, built for leaders. The descriptions have converged, so they no longer help you choose. The questions below do, because the answers still differ enormously between providers.

None of them are trick questions. A provider who has thought about their format will answer each one in a sentence.

## 1. What will I personally have running at the end?

The single most useful question, and the one that separates formats faster than any other. Answers range from "notes and a framework" through "a demo you watched someone build" to "a workflow of your own, running". All three are legitimate products. Only you know which one you are paying for, so make them say which it is.

If the answer is a list of things you will "understand" or "be able to", that is a knowledge product, not a build one.

The word itself is the problem. "Hands-on" covers [four quite different formats](/guides/what-hands-on-ai-training-actually-means/), and this question is usually what settles which one you are being sold.

## 2. Whose work do I build against - mine, or a case study?

A prepared case study is easier to run and much easier to get right. It is also the point at which most training stops transferring, because the hard part of applying AI is the mess in your own work, not the tidy version in the exercise.

Ask whether you bring a real task, and what happens to it during the day. If you do bring one, ask what makes a good one, and take the answer seriously - it usually determines how much you get out of the day.

## 3. How many people are in the room, and who is helping them?

Cohort size decides what the format can actually be. Above roughly thirty people, a day cannot be facilitated in any meaningful sense; it becomes a lecture with exercises, however it is billed. Ask for the cap, not the typical number, and ask how many people are circulating to help.

## 4. What is the ratio of talking to building?

Ask for it in minutes. A day described as hands-on can still be five hours of presentation with a workshop after lunch. A straight answer sounds like a running order: this long building, this long between builds, longest block of anyone talking is this.

## 5. What happens when I get stuck?

Everyone gets stuck, usually on something dull - a login, a permission, a tool that behaves differently on their machine. Whether that costs you ten minutes or the afternoon is a property of the format, not of you. Ask who walks the room.

## 6. What do I need to bring or set up beforehand?

Two things worth knowing early: whether you need a paid AI subscription, and whether any setup is expected before you arrive. Neither is unreasonable. Both are annoying to discover the night before.

## 7. Who else will be in the room?

Peer level matters more in a small room than the syllabus does. A day of founders and directors working on their own operations is a different experience from a mixed room of engineers and analysts, even with identical content. Ask who the day is designed for, and who actually came last time.

## 8. What does the day deliberately not cover?

The most revealing question on this list. Any honest one-day format has a scope decision behind it, and the provider should be able to name what they left out and why. A day that claims to cover the landscape, the tooling, the governance and a build is describing four days.

## 9. What if I book and then cannot make the date?

Diaries move, and this is the question most buyers only think to ask afterwards. The cancellation policy is usually the last line on a course page and the first thing that matters once a client meeting lands on the same morning.

"Transferable" on its own does not tell you much. Three things decide whether it means anything. Does the place move to another date at all, or is the fee simply gone? Is there a charge for moving it, and is that a flat admin fee or a percentage of what you paid? And is the move gated by a notice period - often counted in working days rather than calendar days, which can close the window a week earlier than you would expect.

Ask for the answers in writing before you pay rather than after. If someone else is approving the spend, [they will ask the same thing](/guides/getting-an-ai-bootcamp-approved/), and a clear answer removes the easiest reason to say not yet. It is a small question that tells you how a provider treats the people who have already paid.

## 10. What do I get after the day, and for how long?

The question almost nobody asks until the following week. One-day formats differ more here than anywhere else on this list, and the difference is rarely on the booking page. Some end at 17:00, and that is the whole offer. Others send a written pack afterwards, keep a channel open for a week or two, or book a short call once you have tried the thing on your own work. Those are different products at a similar price.

Two halves of the answer are worth separating. The first is what you take away: the thing you built, in your own hands rather than inside the provider's account, plus whatever is written down so you can rebuild it. The second is what happens when it breaks a fortnight later, which is usually when people actually need the help. Ask for both in writing, because "ongoing support" covers everything from a mailing list to a named person who answers.

A provider who says the day ends on the day has given you a fair answer, and an easier one to plan around than a vague promise. What you want to avoid is finding the boundary at the moment you have crossed it.

## Using the checklist

You do not need perfect answers to all ten. You need answers that are consistent with each other: a room capped small enough for the help promised, a build ratio consistent with the result claimed, a scope narrow enough to be delivered in the time. Most mismatches show up when you put two answers side by side.

For the record, here are ours. We run three one-day bootcamps in Oxford, and several of the ten answers differ between them.

The [Oxford Agentic Bootcamp](/bootcamps/oxford-agentic-bootcamp/) is one day in Oxford, capped at a small room, built around build cycles rather than talks, with no block longer than twenty-five minutes of anyone talking at you. You bring one real task from your own work and a laptop, and you leave with a working agentic workflow running against that task, plus the pattern behind it. No coding is required. A paid AI plan helps but is not essential. The day is not recorded, because the value is in the room. If you cannot make the date, your place transfers to a future cohort at no charge: let us know and we will move you. After the day you keep the workflow and a written recipe for rebuilding and extending it, and you leave with the group: everyone in the room has built the same thing, you go away with their contact details, and you hear first when the next one is on.

What that day deliberately does not cover: the AI landscape, model comparisons, and governance policy. One pattern, applied properly to one real task, is what fits in a day.

The [Oxford Second Brain Bootcamp](/bootcamps/second-brain-bootcamp/) runs in a small, capped group for people who already use AI most days and have hit the ceiling of what prompting alone can do. You bring a laptop and your own material - the documents, notes and examples that carry how you work and what you know - and build a second brain out of that rather than from a template, so you leave with it populated, organised and set up to maintain. Nothing technical is required: the work is thinking clearly about your own business rather than writing code. What it is not is a conceptual overview of AI, a vendor pitch or a tutorial in one app.

The [Oxford Agentic Coding Bootcamp](/bootcamps/agentic-coding-bootcamp/) is for people who understand the concepts but have not yet built and shipped something of their own. It does not assume you can code - Claude Code and Codex do the typing - and what it teaches is everything around the code: the brief the agent reads first, the terminal, version control, deployment, databases and testing, and how to check what the agent wrote rather than trusting it. You leave with a personal website live on the internet and a working prototype that saves real data, both in your own GitHub repository. Question 6 is the one that matters most here: bring a laptop you can install software on, because it involves setting up a code editor, a terminal and a GitHub account on your own machine, and corporate or government devices often block that. If yours is locked down, email hello@oxfordagentic.com before you book and we will work out what you need.

All three run 09:00 to 17:00, in person in Oxford, and all three answer question 9 the same way: places are not refundable, but if the date stops working your place transfers to a future cohort at no charge.
