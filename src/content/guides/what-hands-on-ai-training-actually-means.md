Nearly every AI course now describes itself as hands-on, practical or applied. The word has stretched to cover four quite different experiences, and the difference between them is the difference between a good day out and a changed working week.

## The four things "hands-on" can mean

**1. Watching a live demonstration.** Someone builds something in front of you. Genuinely useful for seeing what is possible - and it is still a talk. Your hands are not on anything.

**2. Following a guided exercise.** You type along with an instructor towards a known destination, usually on prepared material. You finish with the instructor's example, built through your keyboard. Better; the friction is real but the problem is borrowed.

**3. Building against a set brief.** You work on a case or a sample dataset with support in the room. Real building, real decisions - but when you get home, the transfer is still yours to do: your work does not look like the brief.

**4. Building against your own task.** You bring a piece of your actual work - the report, the sift, the weekly assembly job - and build the workflow against that. When the day ends there is nothing to transfer, because the thing you built is already about your job.

Each step up costs more facilitation and caps the room size, which is why most large-format training stops at levels one or two. That is an economic choice, not a dishonest one - but it is worth knowing which one you are buying.

## Three questions that settle it

Course pages rarely say which level they mean, so ask directly:

- **What will I personally have running at the end?** The honest answers range from "notes and ideas" to "a working workflow against your own task". Both are legitimate products; they are different products.
- **Whose problem do I work on?** A demo, a script, a case study, or my own work.
- **What happens when I get stuck?** A forum, office hours, or a person who walks over. The answer predicts whether you finish.

## Why this matters more for senior people

The more senior you are, the scarcer the day and the higher the cost of a format that ends in intentions. An afternoon of demos can be worthwhile for orientation - once. After that, the only training worth a diary slot is the kind measured by what runs afterwards.

For what it is worth, our own format is built deliberately at level four: the [Oxford Agentic Bootcamp](/bootcamps/oxford-agentic-bootcamp/) caps the room at roughly twenty-five, asks everyone to bring one real task, and treats "what will you use this for next week?" as the closing question of the day. No coding, and no stretch of anyone talking at you for more than twenty-five minutes.

The [Oxford Second Brain Bootcamp](/bootcamps/second-brain-bootcamp/) sits at the same level for the same reason. You are asked to bring your own documents and notes, and the day is spent building a second brain out of them rather than out of a supplied example, so what exists at the end is yours and already populated. Level four is not a property of the subject. It is a property of whose material is on the screen.
